# Artifact-embedded distribution

## Mechanism

Most distribution mechanisms examined in this project require the operator to either already have an audience, assemble one from public data, or find buyers who advertise their own need. This one inverts the relationship: the operator gives participants something they want for themselves, and the participants' own act of using or displaying it recruits the next participant from an audience the operator never reaches directly.

The artifact must satisfy one condition to work: it has to say something favourable, or at least noteworthy, about the person holding it. A badge showing a good score. A public page showing you competed and did well. A generated result worth screenshotting. Nobody shares an artifact that makes them look bad or that reflects on the operator instead of on them.

## Why it is separated from every other family in this project

`niche_listing_board` and `public_budget_signal` both require the operator to reach a specific audience directly, by seeding one or by finding buyers who signal themselves. `public_corpus_aggregation` produces distribution as a byproduct of a parsed corpus ranking in search. None of them compound the way this one can: each new participant is also a potential new distribution node, so the channel can grow faster than the operator's own effort scales.

This is also the first family in the project where the proof at scale and the proof at solo scale are genuinely two different companies rather than one instance stretched to cover both claims. `ev_025` establishes the mechanism works at over 100 million dollars ARR, run by a company that was never solo or zero-capital. `ev_024` establishes a solo, zero-capital operator ran the same mechanic and saw it work, at a much smaller and much less durable scale.

## Why it is the higher-risk, higher-upside family in this corpus

Every other mechanism examined caps out at roughly linear returns to founder effort — one more listing, one more digest entry, one more outreach email produces roughly one more unit of value. This family's central claim is that it does not have to: a participant who shares the artifact recruits participants for free, so growth can outpace the founder's own labour.

That same property is what makes it risky. The mechanic either catches on, in which case growth compounds, or it does not, in which case there is no fallback distribution channel the way a parsed corpus still ranks in search even without going viral. `ev_024` is one data point and it describes a spike, not a curve sustained over years.

## How instances differ

**Artifact type** is the central design decision. G2's badge is a static credential; RankInPublic's matchup page is a dynamic, competitive result. A static credential is durable and low-maintenance; a competitive result is more shareable in the moment and decays faster.

**Participant incentive** determines whether the mechanism engages at all. Both instances rely on the participant being a business or founder with something to prove publicly — a private individual with no public stake in the outcome may not share the same way.

**Operator scale** spans the entire range this project has recorded: from a single founder's side project to a company at nine figures of annual revenue, on the same underlying mechanic.

## Open questions

Whether the mechanism works for participants who are not themselves trying to build a public reputation. Both instances involve founders or vendors marketing themselves; a consumer-facing version with no comparable ego stake is untested here.

Whether RankInPublic's growth is durable. The record captures a spike attributed to a mechanic change, not a multi-year trajectory the way G2's is.

What the minimum viable artifact looks like for a category this project has not yet tried it on. Neither instance is in a niche this founder would necessarily be adapting into, so the transfer is untested by category, only by mechanism.
