# Remote Rocketship

## What it does

Collects job openings from the applicant tracking systems most companies already use, parses them for the fields buyers care about, and sells filtered access to the people looking for those jobs.

## How it makes money

The demand side pays, at a few dollars a week. The supply side pays nothing and is never contacted.

This is the inversion run 02 hypothesised in `idea_08` and could not evidence. It is evidenced now.

## Why it matters more than the revenue figure

The revenue is modest. The structure is not.

Three constraints have blocked candidates across every prior run of this engine: no capital, no audience, no relationships. This instance needs none of them at the point of assembly. The inventory is public, free, structured, and continuously refreshed by thousands of parties acting for reasons of their own. Nobody has to be persuaded to supply it.

What remains is a demand-side problem only, and the corpus itself attacks that: thousands of parsed listings become thousands of pages answering queries people are already typing. Distribution is an output of the mechanism rather than an input the founder must bring. Against `ev_019`, which shows the celebrated solo businesses launching into audiences their founders spent years acquiring, this is the material difference.

## What is not clean about it

The first traffic came from a single community post that went viral - a hundred and fifty thousand visitors in a day (`ev_018`). That is an outcome, not a repeatable plan, and a candidate resting on reproducing it is resting on luck. The durable channel is search, and search is slow.

The terms-of-service position on scraping the source systems is unresolved. The record establishes that an operator does this openly at scale. It does not establish that doing so is permitted.

## How it differs from other instances

Against MoAIJobs and RemoteOK: those charge the supply side per listing and therefore need an audience before anyone pays. This one charges the demand side and therefore needs the audience *to be* the customer. The cold-start problem changes shape rather than disappearing - but assembling inventory is no longer part of it.
